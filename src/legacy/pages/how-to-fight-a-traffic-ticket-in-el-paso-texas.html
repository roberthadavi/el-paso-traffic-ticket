How to Fight a Traffic Ticket in El Paso, Texas

Here are the steps to contest an El Paso traffic ticket yourself. The process takes time and more than one trip to court, which is why many drivers hire a traffic ticket lawyer instead — but whether you go it alone or not, these are the steps the court will expect you to follow.

1. Be polite and cooperative when you get pulled over. Being belligerent or indignant may make you feel better, but it tends to cost you later. The officer has discretion over what to write and whether to issue a warning at all, and a hostile stop gets noted on the citation and read by the prosecutor months down the road.

2. Avoid admissions of guilt and do not make excuses or invent a story. When you are asked if you know why you were pulled over, a simple and polite "No, officer, I do not" is enough. Anything you say at the roadside can be written on the back of the citation and used against you if you decide to contest it.

3. Pay attention to how the offense was detected. For a speeding ticket, note where the officer was positioned when your speed was measured and what was used: radar, lidar, or pacing from a patrol car. If the officer paced you, note where the pacing started. Write down the patrol unit number and the officer's name and badge number from the citation. If the citation was for something other than speeding, make sure you understand exactly what the officer says you did, especially if it is something that could not have been easily seen from the officer's position.
The officer does not have to explain the equipment at the roadside. Details such as the device used and its calibration records are requested later through discovery (see step 11).

4. Check your ticket for accuracy as soon as you receive it. There are two considerations here. If there are inaccuracies that may hurt your defense (for example, the officer notes that you crossed two lanes of traffic when you crossed one, or that traffic was heavy when it was light), you may politely ask the officer to correct them. If the officer is not accommodating, do not argue; note the actual circumstances and write them down after the stop. On the other hand, if there are inaccuracies that may help your defense, such as the wrong plate number, the wrong street or the wrong statute, do not call attention to them at the roadside. Whether a particular error matters is something to evaluate with an attorney.

5. Begin preparing your defense immediately, once the officer has left the scene. Record the relevant details while they are fresh: traffic, road conditions, weather, time of day and anything unusual. If you have a phone camera, take pictures, especially if your defense depends on something like an obscured speed limit sign or a pothole you had to swerve to avoid. A dash camera is the best witness you can have; it can show the light, the stop, your speed and the flow of traffic around you. Go back to the officer's original position and check for any obstruction that might have limited the officer's view or affected a radar reading. Make a simple diagram of the road showing where the officer was positioned, which direction you were traveling, where you stopped and other important details.

6. Read the fine print on the ticket after you get home. It tells you which court has your case, the appearance deadline, and how to respond. In Texas the appearance deadline is not optional: if you do not respond by that date, the court can issue a warrant and add a separate charge for violating your promise to appear, and an OMNI hold can block your DPS license renewal until the court clears it. Our traffic warrant pages explain what to do if that has already happened.

7. Identify exactly what you are charged with and which court has it. The statute or code number on the citation tells you the offense. The issuing agency usually tells you the court: El Paso Police citations generally go to El Paso Municipal Court, while sheriff, constable and DPS citations in the county generally go to a Justice of the Peace court.
Then find out what a conviction costs. The fine and court costs are set by the court. Beyond that, paying a Class C citation enters a plea and results in a conviction on your DPS driving record, and a conviction may affect insurance premiums depending on your history, carrier and other rating factors. Texas no longer uses the former Driver Responsibility Program points system, but repeated moving-violation convictions can still lead to a DPS license suspension — see our explainer on what affects your Texas driving record now. CDL holders face separate consequences.

8. Weigh the cost of fighting the ticket against what a conviction would mean for you. Factor in time off work for each court setting, the fine and court costs, the record consequences, and whether a dismissal, a reduction or a deferred disposition is realistically available for your citation.

9. Decide whether you need a lawyer. In Texas Class C courts you have the right to be represented, and an attorney can generally appear on your behalf at pretrial settings so you do not have to miss work. A lawyer who handles the same El Paso courts every week also knows which prosecutors and judges will consider which outcomes. If you want to compare, our fight my ticket page lists the flat fee for your court.

10. Do not simply pay the fine. Your ticket will give you the option to pay, and in Texas paying is a plea that results in a conviction. Instead, enter a plea of not guilty by the appearance date, in person, by mail or online where the court allows it, and ask the clerk how that court schedules a pretrial setting or trial.

11. Get as much information as you can. Well before your court date, send a written request for discovery, the process by which you ask to see the evidence the prosecutor has. How each El Paso court handles discovery in a Class C case varies, so ask the clerk whether a written request or a filed motion is expected. Things you will want to specifically request (and you generally must be specific) include the officer's copy of the citation and any notes, the maintenance and calibration records for any speed-measuring device, the officer's training certification for that device, and any dash or body camera video.
The nature of your case and your planned defense will dictate the exact information you need.

12. Talk to the prosecutor. El Paso courts generally set a pretrial conference before trial. This is the opportunity to ask for a dismissal, a reduction to a non-moving offense or a deferred disposition before you go in front of the judge. Consider any offer carefully, and make sure you understand what it means for your driving record and your insurance before you accept it.

13. Consider the driving safety course. Texas lets eligible drivers ask the court for a driving safety course (defensive driving) under Tex. Code Crim. Proc. art. 45A.352; when you complete it and provide a certified driving record, the citation is dismissed. You must request it at or before your plea, and eligibility is limited: a valid license and insurance, no course in the previous 12 months, and, in general, no CDL and no high-speed citation. Our deferred adjudication and defensive driving page compares this with deferred disposition.

14. Request a reset if you need one. If you have a legitimate conflict, such as work, a witness who is unavailable, or discovery you have not yet received, ask the court for a continuance in writing and well before the setting. At trial the State needs the officer to testify; if the officer does not appear the court may dismiss or may reset the case, so do not build your plan around an officer no-show.

15. Plan your defense. The State has to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt: that you were the driver, the date and location, and the specific violation charged. If the officer's testimony leaves out an element, that is a ground for dismissal, but only if you catch it, which is where an attorney earns the fee. Once you have decided to go to trial, know how you will argue your case. A significant error on the ticket may support a dismissal, but minor discrepancies, such as the color of your car, will not. If your defense rests on circumstances, make sure they actually answer the charge: "I was running late to work" will not impress the judge. Make an outline of your points and keep your evidence organized.

16. Go to court and plead not guilty. Show up for your setting looking clean and professional. If you have not yet had a chance to speak to the prosecutor, now is the time. Unless there is an acceptable offer, maintain your not guilty plea. If you decide not to go to trial, ask about deferred disposition rather than simply pleading no contest: a plain no-contest plea is a conviction on your record, while a completed deferred disposition is not. Many El Paso law firms will handle these settings for a flat fee, saving you the time and lost wages of going to court yourself.

17. Use facts to present your case to the judge without admitting guilt. "I was only doing 57 in a 55" is an admission. "I was traveling at a safe speed for the conditions" is not. Politely and clearly explain your defense, entering your photos, diagram and any records as evidence. In some situations (a malfunctioning signal, an obscured speed limit sign) you may be able to acknowledge what happened without harming your case, but think that through before you say it. Ignorance of a posted limit or a local ordinance is generally not a defense.

18. Make notes of the judge's decision. Class C proceedings are not routinely transcribed, and there can be ambiguity in how the judge announces the result and any conditions. Before you leave, confirm with the clerk that what is entered in the court's system matches what the judge said.

19. Follow through on everything the court requires. If the judge grants deferred disposition or a driving safety course, the dismissal depends on you meeting every condition and deadline: paying the costs the court sets, completing the course, filing the certificate and driving record, and picking up no new violations during the deferral period. If you miss a deadline, the court can enter the conviction, and a missed payment or appearance can lead to a warrant.

20. Order your DPS driver record afterward. Occasionally a clerical error causes a dismissed citation to appear on your record as a conviction, and these are difficult to fix after an insurer has already acted on them. A few weeks after your case closes, order your record from DPS online and confirm it is accurate. If it is not, call the court's clerk's office and ask them to verify that the case shows as dismissed, paid or deferred, whichever applies, and to correct what was reported.
