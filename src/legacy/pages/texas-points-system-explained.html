The Texas Points System Was Repealed — What Affects Your El Paso Driving Record Now

If you searched for the "Texas points system," here is the short version: Texas no longer has one. The Driver Responsibility Program — the state program that attached points to traffic convictions and billed drivers an annual surcharge — was repealed by the Texas Legislature effective September 1, 2019. Any article that still describes two points for a moving violation, three points for a crash, or a yearly surcharge once you reached six points is describing a program that ended.

That does not mean an El Paso traffic ticket is harmless. This page covers two things:

  1. What the repeal ended — the point assessments and the surcharges, and
  2. What the repeal did not touch — the convictions themselves, which still go on your Texas Department of Public Safety (DPS) driving record and still have consequences.

For the historical record only: under the former program, DPS assessed points for each traffic conviction, billed a surcharge every year a driver carried six or more points, and billed separate annual surcharges for convictions such as DWI, no insurance, driving while license invalid and no driver's license. None of that is assessed today.

DPS answers the most common questions about the repeal on its Driver Responsibility Program surcharge repeal FAQ.

What happened to drivers who still owed surcharges

According to DPS, surcharge assessments and installment plans were waived as of September 1, 2019, and license suspensions that were based only on unpaid surcharges were lifted. Payments made before that date were not refunded. Two cautions apply:

  1. A surcharge-related suspension remains in your driving history even though it is no longer active.
  2. The repeal covered surcharges only. A suspension for any other reason, unpaid court fines, reinstatement fees and failure-to-appear holds were not waived and still have to be resolved on their own.

NOTE: If your license was suspended before 2019 and you never confirmed it came back, do not assume it is valid. Driving on a license that is still suspended for a different reason is a separate offense, driving while license invalid, and it adds a new conviction to the record you are trying to protect.

Before you drive, do two things:

  1. Check your current license status through the DPS online eligibility check (you will need your license number, date of birth and the last four digits of your Social Security number).
  2. If the status shows a hold or an unpaid reinstatement fee, find out which court or program it belongs to. Our driver's license help page explains the usual causes and how an attorney clears them.

What still matters: the conviction on your driving record

In Texas, paying a Class C traffic citation is not just paying a bill. It enters a plea, and the result is a conviction that the court reports to DPS. DPS keeps that conviction on your driving record, and that record is what insurers, employers and DPS itself look at. The repeal of the points system removed the surcharge attached to a conviction; it did not remove the conviction.

Convictions are added once DPS receives notice from the court, so there can be a lag between the court date and the entry on your record. If you want to know what is already there, you can order your own driver record from DPS online.

Insurance

A traffic conviction may affect insurance premiums depending on the driver's history, carrier and other rating factors. There is no fixed increase and no state formula; each carrier applies its own underwriting rules when it reviews your record at renewal. A dismissal, or a deferred disposition that you complete, is designed to keep the conviction off the record the carrier sees.

DPS suspension for habitual violators

Separate from the repealed program, Texas Transportation Code §521.292 directs DPS to suspend the license of a driver it determines to be a "habitual violator." In general, that means a driver with:

  1. Four or more moving-violation convictions arising from different incidents within 12 consecutive months, or
  2. Seven or more moving-violation convictions arising from different incidents within 24 months.

Out-of-state convictions count toward the total. Certain offenses, such as seat-belt violations and commercial vehicle weight limits, are excluded by the statute. Drivers under 18 face a lower threshold under the same section. The practical point is simple: the points are gone, but a run of convictions in a short period can still cost you your license, and a dismissed or deferred ticket does not count as a conviction.

CDL holders

If you hold a commercial driver's license, the repeal changed less for you than for anyone else. Federal and Texas commercial-driver rules still track moving-violation convictions, still carry disqualification consequences for serious violations, and still prohibit "masking" — which is why Texas courts generally cannot grant deferred disposition or the driving safety course to a CDL holder, even for a ticket received in a personal vehicle. For a CDL driver, dismissal or a reduction to a non-moving offense is usually the only outcome that protects the license. See our CDL and commercial driver ticket defense page.

  1. Do not pay a CDL citation online to "get it over with" — payment is a conviction.
  2. Do not assume a ticket in your personal car stays off your commercial record; Texas reports convictions to the driver, not the vehicle.
  3. Tell your attorney about every prior conviction, including out-of-state, before the first court setting.
  4. Bring your medical certificate and employer requirements into the conversation early; some employers act on a conviction before DPS does.

Why deferred disposition and defensive driving matter more now

With the point system gone, the question for an El Paso ticket is no longer "how many points is this?" but "will this become a conviction on my record?" Texas courts have two discretionary tools that can answer no: deferred disposition under Tex. Code Crim. Proc. art. 45A.302, and the driving safety course (defensive driving) under art. 45A.352. Both have eligibility limits — CDL holders, higher speeds in some courts and no-insurance citations are common exclusions — and both must be requested before you enter a plea and pay. Our deferred adjudication and defensive driving page compares the two and explains how each El Paso court tends to handle them.

  1. Deferred disposition: you pay court costs and a special expense fee set by the court, complete any conditions, and stay violation-free for the deferral period; the case is then dismissed and no conviction is reported.
  2. Driving safety course: you complete an approved course within the court's deadline and provide a certified driving record; the citation is dismissed on proof of completion.
  3. Dismissal on the merits: when the citation, the stop or the evidence has a problem, we pursue an outright dismissal — see how traffic tickets get dismissed.
  4. Ignoring the ticket: a missed appearance date can lead to a warrant and a separate charge, and an OMNI hold that blocks your license renewal — see our traffic warrant pages.

  • A photo of the citation (front and back), your driver's license and your insurance card, and
  • Any court notice or letter you have received, plus a note of any prior tickets in the last two years, in or out of Texas.

Checking your El Paso Municipal Court case

If your ticket was issued inside the City of El Paso, the Municipal Court lists pending citations, appearance dates and amounts due on its public case search. If it was issued by the sheriff, a constable or DPS in the county, it is likely in a Justice of the Peace court instead; our traffic ticket defense page explains how to tell which court has your case.

You can look up a City of El Paso citation on the Municipal Court public case search. Treat the amount shown as the price of a conviction, not as the only way to close the case.
